CQC dental inspection checklist: every answer ready before they ask.

The questions inspectors ask dental practices, and the evidence that answers each one, set out under CQC’s five key questions. Written by dental care professionals, including a former CQC Registered Manager.

The call comes. Two weeks to go.

That’s the usual notice CQC gives a dental practice. Two weeks isn’t long enough to build a year of evidence. It is long enough to check it.

This checklist is how you check it. It follows the five questions CQC asks of every practice: is it safe, effective, caring, responsive and well-led? Under each one you’ll find the evidence an inspector looks for, and the questions they’re likely to ask you and your team.

Work through it now, not when the call comes. Then inspection day is a calm walk-through of what you already do.

Most practices that fail don’t fail on the dentistry. In CQC’s published data from 1 October 2026, 85 of the 86 dental practices with a “not all regulations met” key question were marked down on well-led.

What should a CQC dental inspection checklist cover?

A CQC dental inspection checklist for a practice in England should cover the evidence behind each of CQC’s five key questions. For safe, that means medical emergency drugs and equipment, decontamination to HTM 01-05, Legionella and waterline control, X-ray safety under IRR17 and IR(ME)R 2017, fire safety, COSHH, sharps, safeguarding and complete staff files. For effective, it means clinical records, consent, prevention, training, CPD and clinical audits. For caring and responsive, it means privacy, patient feedback, complaints, access and urgent care. For well-led, it means governance: every check and audit with a named owner and a due date, an action plan for every gap, and a Registered Manager who can explain it all. Each item should show when it was last done, when it is next due and who owns it.

1
Key question one

Safe: are people protected from harm?

Medical emergencies

  • Emergency drugs and oxygen in date, checked and the check recorded
  • AED, airway kit and suction ready, with a named person checking (and cover when they’re off)
  • Whole-team medical emergency training at least once a year

Resuscitation Council UK quality standards

Decontamination

  • Daily and weekly tests on autoclaves and washer-disinfectors, logged
  • Equipment serviced and validated on time
  • Infection control audit every 6 months, with an action plan

HTM 01-05

Water and Legionella

  • Legionella risk assessment by a competent person, kept up to date
  • Monthly hot and cold water temperature checks, recorded
  • Dental unit waterlines flushed, treated and tested as your plan says

HSE L8 · HTM 04-01 · HTM 01-05

X-rays

  • Practice registered with HSE; Radiation Protection Adviser appointed
  • Radiation Protection Supervisor named; local rules by each unit
  • Equipment tests, quality assurance and a radiation file in order
  • Every X-ray justified, graded and reported in the notes

IRR17 · IR(ME)R 2017

Fire safety

  • Written fire risk assessment, reviewed
  • Alarm tests, emergency lighting checks and drills, logged
  • Extinguishers serviced; escape routes clear

Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005

Hazardous substances and sharps

  • COSHH assessment and safety data sheet for each product
  • Sharps risk assessment and safer sharps where practicable
  • Everyone knows the needlestick steps

COSHH 2002 · Sharps Regulations 2013

Safeguarding

  • A named safeguarding lead
  • Training at the right level for every role
  • Local contact numbers easy to find

Regulation 13

Staff files

  • ID, DBS check, references and full work history (gaps explained)
  • GDC registration and indemnity for every registrant
  • Hepatitis B immunity for clinical staff

Regulation 19 and Schedule 3

Equipment and waste

  • Written scheme of examination for the compressor and autoclaves
  • Clinical waste segregated, stored safely and consignment notes kept

PSSR 2000 · HTM 07-01

What an inspector may ask
  • “Show me how you check the emergency drugs. Who does it when that person is on holiday?”
  • “When was your last infection control audit, and what did you change after it?”
  • “Talk me through what happens if a nurse has a needlestick injury.”
  • “Who is your Radiation Protection Adviser?”
  • “How would you raise a safeguarding concern about a patient?”
2
Key question two

Effective: does care get good results?

Clinical records

  • Medical history updated at every course of treatment
  • Consent, options and risks written down
  • Recall interval set by risk, not habit

NICE CG19 · Regulation 11

Prevention

  • Advice follows Delivering Better Oral Health
  • Fluoride and smoking advice recorded where it applies

DHSC Delivering Better Oral Health

Training and CPD

  • A tracker for every registrant’s CPD and personal development plan
  • Induction records for every new starter
  • Appraisals done and dated

GDC enhanced CPD · Regulation 18

Clinical audits

  • X-ray quality audit
  • Record-keeping audit
  • Antibiotic prescribing audit, each with actions

IR(ME)R 2017 · Regulation 17

Consent and capacity

  • Team understands the Mental Capacity Act 2005
  • Team knows how Gillick competence applies to young patients

Mental Capacity Act 2005

What an inspector may ask
  • “How do you decide how often a patient should come back?”
  • “How do you know everyone’s training is up to date?”
  • “Show me your last X-ray audit. What did it find?”
3
Key question three

Caring: do patients feel looked after?

Dignity and privacy

  • Private conversations away from the waiting room
  • Screens and records kept out of sight
  • Chaperones offered

Regulation 10

Patient voice

  • Feedback collected (Friends and Family Test for NHS care)
  • Feedback read and acted on, with “you said, we did” examples

Regulation 9

Nervous patients

  • A clear way of supporting anxious patients that the whole team can describe

Regulation 9

What an inspector may ask
  • “How do you support a patient who is very nervous?”
  • “How do you keep conversations private at reception?”
4
Key question four

Responsive: does care fit people’s needs?

Complaints

  • A written complaints procedure patients can see
  • A complaints log with replies on time
  • What changed because of each one

Regulation 16

Access

  • Reasonable adjustments made and recorded
  • Disability access assessment done
  • Interpreters available; information in accessible formats

Equality Act 2010 · Accessible Information Standard

Urgent care and information

  • How patients get an urgent appointment, and what to do out of hours
  • Clear fees and treatment options before treatment starts

Regulation 9

What an inspector may ask
  • “Show me your last complaint. What changed because of it?”
  • “How would a wheelchair user get into a surgery?”
  • “A patient rings in pain at 5pm on a Friday. What happens?”
5
Key question five

Well-led: can you prove it’s all happening?

Governance

  • Every check and audit has a named owner and a due date
  • Gaps go on an action plan and get closed
  • A risk register that’s actually used

Regulation 17

Leadership

  • Registered Manager in post and knows the role
  • Statement of purpose up to date
  • Team meetings minuted

Health and Social Care Act 2008 Regulations

Openness

  • Duty of candour understood and recorded when used
  • Significant events logged and learned from
  • Staff know how to speak up

Regulation 20

Information

  • ICO registration in place; UK GDPR training done
  • Data Security and Protection Toolkit submitted if you hold an NHS contract

UK GDPR · Regulation 17

What an inspector may ask
  • “How do you know everything in this practice is being done?”
  • “What are your biggest risks, and what are you doing about them?”
  • “What has changed since your last inspection?”

Inspection day: calm, not cramming.

  • One place for everything. A folder or screen, in the order of the five key questions, so any answer is one tap away.
  • A named greeter. Someone who knows the inspector is coming, has a room ready and knows who to fetch.
  • The Registered Manager interview. Expect questions on how you know things are done, your risks and your last audits.
  • Team chats. Inspectors speak to nurses and reception too. A quick run-through of the questions above helps nerves.
  • The walk-round. Surgeries, the decontamination room, the emergency kit and the waste store.
  • Honest about gaps. A gap with an owner, a date and an action plan shows good governance. A hidden gap doesn’t.

Questions practices ask us.

What does CQC check in a dental practice inspection?

CQC checks whether a dental practice in England meets the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014 by asking five key questions: is the practice safe, effective, caring, responsive to people's needs, and well-led. Inspectors look at three kinds of evidence: what the practice's records show, what staff say when they are interviewed, and what patients say. In a typical dental inspection the inspector walks round the surgeries and decontamination room, checks the emergency drugs and equipment, reads staff files, audits and risk assessments, and interviews the Registered Manager and members of the team. Safe and well-led are where most of the written evidence sits, so expect much of the visit to focus there.

What questions do CQC inspectors ask dental practices?

CQC inspectors ask dental practices open questions that test whether the team knows and follows its own systems. Common examples are: how do you check the emergency drugs and who covers when that person is away; when was your last infection control audit and what did you change; what happens after a needlestick injury; who is your Radiation Protection Adviser; how would you raise a safeguarding concern; how do you decide a patient's recall interval; how do you know everyone's training is up to date; show me your last complaint and what changed; and how do you know everything in the practice is being done. Inspectors ask nurses and receptionists as well as the dentists and the Registered Manager, so the whole team should be comfortable answering.

How much notice does CQC give before a dental inspection?

CQC usually gives a dental practice about two weeks' notice of an inspection, so that patient care is disrupted as little as possible. CQC can also inspect without notice, for example when it has received a concern about a practice. Two weeks is enough time to check that evidence is in order, but not enough to create a year of records, audits and checks from scratch. That is why practices that do well keep their checks dated and up to date all year rather than preparing only when the call comes.

Do dental practices get a CQC rating?

Most dental practices in England are not given a CQC rating such as Good or Outstanding. Instead, for each key question that is looked at, the inspection report says either that the practice is meeting the regulations or that not all regulations are met. Where regulations are not met, CQC can issue a requirement notice asking the practice to send an action plan, or take stronger enforcement action such as a warning notice. The report is published on the CQC website, where patients can read it.

What is the most common reason dental practices fail a CQC inspection?

The most common reason dental practices are found not to meet the regulations is well-led, which is about governance. In CQC's published data from 1 October 2026, 86 dental practices in England had at least one key question where not all regulations were met, and 85 of those 86 were marked down on well-led, with 26 marked down on safe. Well-led failures usually come from missing evidence rather than poor dentistry: audits that were not done or not acted on, staff files with gaps, risk assessments out of date, and no clear owner for each check. Regulation 17 (good governance) is the regulation most closely linked to well-led. Read why in our compliance guide.

How often should a dental practice do an infection control audit?

Under HTM 01-05, the Department of Health's guidance on decontamination in primary care dental practices in England, a dental practice should complete an infection prevention and control audit every six months. Many practices use the Infection Prevention Society dental audit tool. The audit should lead to a written action plan with named owners and dates, and inspectors often ask what changed after the last one. HTM 01-05 also sets out daily and weekly testing of decontamination equipment such as autoclaves and washer-disinfectors, with results kept in a logbook.

What emergency drugs and equipment does a dental practice need?

Resuscitation Council UK's quality standards for primary dental care say every practice should have immediate access to oxygen, airway equipment including suction, and an automated external defibrillator (AED), along with drugs to manage medical emergencies such as anaphylaxis, asthma, hypoglycaemia, angina and seizures, following the dental section of the British National Formulary. The drugs usually include adrenaline, aspirin, glucagon, oral glucose, buccal midazolam, GTN spray and a salbutamol inhaler. Named people should check the drugs and equipment regularly, with cover arranged when they are away, and the checks should be recorded. The whole team should have medical emergency training at least once a year.

Is CQC changing how it inspects dental practices in 2026?

Yes, CQC is changing its assessment approach. In March 2026 CQC published draft sector-specific assessment frameworks for consultation, including one for primary care and community services, which covers dental practices. The drafts bring back key lines of enquiry in place of the 34 quality statements of the single assessment framework. The five key questions, safe, effective, caring, responsive and well-led, stay the same, and the regulations practices must meet do not change. The evidence in this checklist is what the regulations and national guidance require, so it stays useful whichever framework is in force. Check cqc.org.uk for the version that applies on the date of your inspection.

Does this checklist apply to practices in Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland?

No. CQC regulates dental practices in England only. Practices in Wales are regulated by Healthcare Inspectorate Wales, practices in Scotland by Healthcare Improvement Scotland and the NHS boards, and practices in Northern Ireland by the Regulation and Quality Improvement Authority. Much of the underlying law, such as the fire safety, COSHH and ionising radiation rules, is similar across the UK, but each nation has its own standards and decontamination guidance, such as WHTM 01-05 in Wales.

What does well-led mean for a small dental practice?

Well-led means the practice can show that its systems work: every check, audit and renewal has a named owner and a due date, gaps are found and fixed, risks are written down and acted on, the team is listened to, and leaders know what is happening. For a small practice this does not need to be complicated. A simple tracker of what is due, who does it and when it was last done, a short action plan after each audit, minuted team meetings, and a Registered Manager who can explain all of this calmly is what inspectors look for. The checklist on this page uses Last done, Next due and Who columns for exactly this reason.
