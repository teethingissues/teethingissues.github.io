Dental decontamination room checklist: clean, tested and ready to show.

One room tells the inspector how your whole practice runs.

The decontamination room is on every CQC walk-round. In a few minutes it shows whether your checks happen every day, or only when someone remembers.

The good news? Everything an inspector looks for here is written down in one place: HTM 01-05, the Department of Health guidance on decontamination in primary care dental practices in England.

This checklist turns it into lines you can tick. Each one is marked Essential (every practice should meet it now) or Best practice (where HTM 01-05 asks you to have a plan to get to).

Work through it once with your decontamination lead. Then every day in that room is calm, and so is inspection day.

What should a dental decontamination room checklist cover?

A dental decontamination room checklist for a practice in England should follow HTM 01-05 (2013 edition). It should cover the layout and its one-way dirty-to-clean flow; the start-of-day steriliser tests; cleaning by washer-disinfector, ultrasonic or by hand at 45°C or lower; inspection under an illuminated magnifier; wrapping and steam sterilising; storage times; end-of-day shut-down; weekly, quarterly and yearly equipment testing and validation; the written scheme of examination for each steriliser under the Pressure Systems Safety Regulations 2000; and the people and records behind it all: a named lead, training, hepatitis B immunity, COSHH assessments and an infection control audit every six months. Each line should show when it was last done, when it is next due and who owns it.

The decontamination process: one way, dirty to clean.

Every instrument makes the same journey. It should never travel backwards, and clean should never meet dirty.

Dirty zoneClean zone
  1. TransportFrom the surgery in a rigid, leak-proof, lidded box marked for dirty instruments, as soon as practical.
  2. CleanIn a washer-disinfector (best practice), or an ultrasonic cleaner with the lid closed, or by hand at 45°C or lower.
  3. RinseIn a separate sink or bowl from the wash, never the hand-wash basin. Not needed after a washer-disinfector.
  4. InspectUnder an illuminated magnifier. Not clean? Clean it again. Damaged or rusty? Take it out.
  5. WrapBefore sterilising in a vacuum (type B or S) steriliser. After sterilising and cooling if your steriliser is type N.
  6. SteriliseIn a validated steam steriliser that passed today’s tests. A failed cycle means those instruments are not used.
  7. StoreDry and covered. Wrapped: up to 1 year. Unwrapped: 1 day in the surgery, 1 week in a clean non-clinical area.
  8. UseCheck the pack is intact before it is opened for the patient. Then the journey starts again.

Cleaning in the surgery? HTM 01-05 allows it under the essential quality requirements, as far from the chair as possible and still dirty to clean. Manual washing, an ultrasonic cleaner without a lid, and opening decontamination equipment should not happen while a patient is in the room.

HTM 01-05 paragraphs 2.4, 2.25, 3.30–3.49, 4.22–4.29, 5.1–5.3 and Chapter 16

1
Section one

The room: built for one-way traffic.

Layout and flow

  • A designated decontamination area with a clear dirty-to-clean route Essential
  • Clean and dirty kept apart by space, or by time with a surface clean between stages Essential
  • A separate room, or a dirty room and a clean room, used only for decontamination Best practice

HTM 01-05 1.8, 2.4j, 5.1–5.7

Sinks and basins

  • A wash-hand basin just for hands Essential
  • Two sinks for washing and rinsing instruments (or one sink with a removable rinse bowl) Essential
  • Instrument sinks never used for hand-washing Essential

HTM 01-05 2.4g, 2.4u, 2.4v

Surfaces

  • Worktops impervious, easy to clean and jointless or sealed Essential
  • Flooring impervious and coved to the wall; no carpet Essential
  • Decluttered: nothing out that isn’t part of the process Essential

HTM 01-05 5.6, 6.46–6.47

2
Section two

Start of day: test before you trust.

Steriliser

  • Door seal wiped, chamber and shelves checked Essential
  • Reservoir filled with distilled or RO water Essential
  • Automatic control test run; temperature and pressure recorded Essential
  • Steam penetration test (Helix or Bowie-Dick) on vacuum sterilisers Essential
  • Results dated and signed in the logbook before first use Essential

HTM 01-05 4.11, 4.15–4.21

Washer-disinfector and ultrasonic

  • Strainers and filters removed and cleaned Essential
  • Any start-up checks in the manufacturer’s instructions done Essential

HTM 01-05 Chapters 13 and 14

Protective kit

  • Disposable gloves (or household gloves for manual cleaning, washed after use and replaced weekly) Essential
  • Single-use plastic apron for all decontamination work Essential
  • Single-use mask and eye protection or a visor Essential

HTM 01-05 6.23–6.29

3
Section three

Cleaning: the step everything else depends on.

Before cleaning

  • Separate, marked boxes for dirty and for sterile instruments, never swapped Essential
  • Single-use items (the crossed-out 2 symbol) thrown away, never reprocessed Essential
  • Endodontic files and reamers used once, or for one patient only Essential

HTM 01-05 2.13–2.19, 2.25–2.27

Washer-disinfector

  • A validated washer-disinfector used as the main cleaning method Best practice
  • Loaded the way that has been shown to clean: hinges open, nothing overlapping Essential

HTM 01-05 1.8, 3.13–3.19

Ultrasonic cleaner

  • Instruments in a basket, fully covered, never on the floor of the tank Essential
  • Lid closed until the cycle ends Essential
  • Solution changed when heavily soiled, and at the end of every session Essential

HTM 01-05 2.4h, 3.30

Cleaning by hand

  • A written procedure everyone has been trained in Essential
  • Instrument detergent only: no hand scrub, washing-up liquid or soap Essential
  • Water checked at 45°C or lower, instruments scrubbed under the water with soft long-handled brushes Essential
  • Rinsed in the second sink; solution and rinse water replaced after each use Essential

HTM 01-05 3.33–3.37, Chapter 16

4
Section four

Inspect, wrap, sterilise: proof it worked.

Inspection

  • Every instrument checked under an illuminated magnifier Essential
  • Not clean: cleaned again. Damaged or rusty: taken out of use Essential

HTM 01-05 2.4h, 3.18, 3.41

Wrapping

  • Dried and wrapped before a vacuum (type B or S) cycle Essential
  • Never wrapped before a type N cycle: wrap after cooling Essential

HTM 01-05 2.4k, 4.9, 4.22

Every cycle

  • A record of every cycle kept (print-out, data-logger or logbook) Essential
  • A failed cycle or test: steriliser out of use, those instruments not used Essential

HTM 01-05 4.3, 4.21

5
Section five

Storage and end of day: nothing left to chance overnight.

Storage times

  • Wrapped instruments dated and used within 1 year Essential
  • Unwrapped in the surgery: 1 day, then reprocessed, used or not Essential
  • Unwrapped in a clean non-clinical area: 1 week Essential
  • Kept dry, covered, off open worktops and above floor level Essential
  • A dedicated store away from the treatment area, in the clean side of the decontamination room Best practice

HTM 01-05 1.8, 2.4k–m, 4.25–4.29

End of day

  • Steriliser drained, cleaned, dried and left empty with the door open Essential
  • Ultrasonic cleaner drained Essential
  • Unwrapped surgery instruments reprocessed (tonight, or first thing tomorrow) Essential
  • Surfaces cleaned, ready for a clean start Essential

HTM 01-05 2.4k, 4.11, Chapter 14

Equipment testing: what’s due, and how often.

HTM 01-05 sets out a schedule for each machine. Each step includes the ones before it, so the weekly test is the daily tests plus the weekly ones. Always add any extra tests in your manufacturer’s instructions.

Quarterly tests follow the manufacturer’s recommendations where they differ. Every machine is validated when it is installed, every year after that, and after any major repair. Daily and weekly records are signed by the named “User” before the machine goes back into use.

Which guidance applies to your practice?

This checklist is for practices in England, where HTM 01-05 (2013 edition) applies and CQC inspects. Practices in Scotland follow SHTM 01-05, practices in Wales follow WHTM 01-05, and practices in Northern Ireland should follow their own regulator’s guidance. The steps look similar, but the details differ, so check the version for your nation.

Questions practices ask us.

What is the dental decontamination process?

The dental decontamination process is the one-way journey a reusable instrument makes from the patient back to the surgery, so that it is safe to use again. Under HTM 01-05, the Department of Health guidance for primary care dental practices in England, the steps are: transport the used instruments in a rigid, leak-proof, lidded container; clean them in a washer-disinfector, an ultrasonic cleaner or by hand; rinse them if they were cleaned by hand or ultrasonic; inspect each one under an illuminated magnifier; wrap them if a vacuum steriliser is used; sterilise them in a validated steam steriliser; store them dry and protected; and check the pack is intact before use. The work should always move from dirty to clean, so used instruments never come into contact with sterilised ones.

Does a dental practice need a separate decontamination room?

Not to meet the essential quality requirements of HTM 01-05. A practice can decontaminate instruments in a designated area in, or next to, a treatment room, as long as the area is as far from the dental chair as practical, the work follows a dirty-to-clean flow, and steps that create splashes or aerosols (manual washing, an ultrasonic cleaner without a lid, opening decontamination equipment) do not happen while a patient is in the room. HTM 01-05 describes a separate room, or a dirty room and a clean room, used only for decontamination and only by the staff doing it, as best practice. Every practice is expected to have a written plan showing how it will move towards best practice.

What tests does a dental autoclave need each day and each week?

HTM 01-05 says a benchtop steam steriliser needs two daily tests before it is used: an automatic control test, which applies to every type, and a steam penetration test such as a Helix or Bowie-Dick test, which applies to vacuum sterilisers (types B and S). The temperature and pressure reached should be recorded in the logbook with the date and the operator's signature. Each week, the daily tests are done along with an air leakage test on vacuum sterilisers, plus any other weekly tests the manufacturer specifies. Quarterly thermometric tests and a full yearly validation are carried out by a Competent Person (Decontamination) or service engineer. If a steriliser fails a test, it should be taken out of use and anything processed in a failed cycle should not be used.

How long can sterilised dental instruments be stored?

Under HTM 01-05, wrapped sterilised instruments can be stored for up to one year, and practices should have a system, such as dating each pack, so that the year is never exceeded. Unwrapped instruments kept in a clinical area can be stored for a maximum of one day, and should be reprocessed at the end of the working day (or the start of the next), whether or not they were used. Unwrapped instruments kept in a clean, non-clinical area, such as the clean side of a separate decontamination room, can be stored for up to one week. In every case instruments should be dry and protected, for example in covered drawer inserts or racks in cupboards, never on open worktops.

What temperature should the water be for manual cleaning of dental instruments?

HTM 01-05 says the water used for manual cleaning of dental instruments should be 45°C or lower, checked with a mercury-free thermometer throughout cleaning, because hotter water makes protein coagulate and stick to the instrument. Use a detergent made for the manual cleaning of dental instruments at the strength the manufacturer gives, never a chlorhexidine hand scrub, washing-up liquid, cleaning cream or soap. Instruments should be fully submerged and scrubbed under the water with soft, long-handled plastic brushes to avoid splashes and aerosols, then rinsed in a second sink, never the hand-wash basin. Manual cleaning is accepted under the essential quality requirements, but it is hard to validate, so a washer-disinfector is the best practice method.

How often should a dental practice do an infection control audit?

HTM 01-05 says every dental practice in England should audit its decontamination processes at least every six months, and strongly recommends the Infection Prevention Society and Department of Health dental audit tool. Each audit should lead to a review and an action plan, with named owners and dates for any gaps. Audit documents should be kept for at least two years, should not be removed from the premises or destroyed, and should be available to CQC on inspection. Inspectors often ask what the last audit found and what changed because of it.

What is the difference between essential quality requirements and best practice in HTM 01-05?

The essential quality requirements are the level every dental practice in England is expected to meet now: instruments cleaned so they are free of visible contamination, reprocessed through a validated cycle including a validated steam steriliser, stored safely with controlled storage times, a decontamination audit every six months, and a written plan for moving towards best practice. Best practice goes further in three main areas: cleaning in a validated washer-disinfector, carrying out decontamination in a separate room or rooms away from patient treatment, and storing sterilised instruments in a dedicated store separate from the treatment area, usually in the clean area of the decontamination room. Practices can reach best practice straight away or step by step through a documented plan.

Does a dental autoclave need a written scheme of examination?

Yes. HTM 01-05 says all steam sterilisers are covered by the Pressure Systems Safety Regulations 2000 and must be examined periodically by a Competent Person. Under these regulations, a written scheme of examination must be in place before the equipment is used, drawn up or certified by a competent person, and each examination must be followed by a written report. In dental practices this service is often provided through the practice's insurer. Keep the written scheme and the examination reports with each steriliser's logbook, as CQC inspectors may ask to see them.

Can endodontic files and reamers be reused?

HTM 01-05 says that where endodontic reamers and files are designated reusable, they should be treated as single use or single patient use, whatever the manufacturer's designation, to reduce the risk of prion transmission. If they are used again for the same patient, the practice must have a reliable way of linking each instrument to that patient. Any device marked single use, shown by a crossed-out number 2 symbol on the packaging, should be used once and thrown away, never reprocessed, even for the same patient at a later visit. HTM 01-05 also suggests replacing hard-to-clean items, such as matrix bands, saliva ejectors, aspirator tips and three-in-one tips, with single-use versions where possible.

How long should decontamination records be kept?

HTM 01-05 recommends that decontamination records are kept for at least two years. That includes a record of every sterilisation cycle (a print-out, data-logger record or logbook entry, copied if the print-out fades), the daily and weekly test results signed by the named User before the machine goes back into use, quarterly and yearly test and validation reports, maintenance and repair records, training records, and the written scheme of examination and examination reports for each steriliser. Infection control audits should also be kept for at least two years and not removed from the premises.
