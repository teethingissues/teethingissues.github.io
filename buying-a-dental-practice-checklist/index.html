Buying a dental practice checklist: buy it with your eyes open.

Every check to make before you sign, the questions to ask the seller, the red flags to walk away from, and a calm plan for your first 90 days. Written by dental care professionals, including a former CQC Registered Manager.

You’ve found the one. Now look under the bonnet.

Congratulations, and well done for getting this far. Finding a practice you love is the exciting part. What happens next decides whether your first year feels calm or chaotic.

Many surprises after a purchase were sitting in the paperwork all along: an NHS contract that has been under-delivering, a CQC application started too late, a team member whose contract nobody read, a lease with three years left.

This checklist puts all of it in front of you before you sign. Work through it with your dental solicitor and accountant, ask the seller every question, and you’ll walk in on day one knowing exactly what you’ve bought.

This is general guidance for practices in England, not legal or financial advice. Speak to a specialist dental solicitor and accountant before you commit.

What should a checklist for buying a dental practice cover?

A checklist for buying a dental practice in England should cover six areas. Regulation: the buyer’s CQC registration (a new provider must register, usually through CQC’s sale and transfer process) and a registered manager where one is required. The NHS contract, if there is one: how it moves to the buyer (a contract novation needs the commissioner’s agreement), recent delivery against contracted UDAs and any money owed back. People: GDC registration for every clinician, TUPE for employed staff and the terms of each associate agreement. Premises: the lease, equipment, and X-ray registration with HSE. Patients and data: how records transfer and the buyer’s own ICO data protection fee. Money: fee income, associate costs, NHS and private mix, and an independent valuation of the goodwill. Each item should be requested, received and checked by a named person before exchange.

Start your DBS check early. CQC will reject a registration application without an enhanced DBS check, and rejects any over 12 months old. If you are not a registered healthcare professional, you need a CQC countersigned check, which CQC says can take up to 60 working days.

Assets or shares? Decide this first.

How you buy changes almost every check that follows, so ask your solicitor early.

Asset purchase

You buy the business (goodwill, equipment and often the lease) from the seller, who could be an individual, a partnership or a company. The provider changes, so you apply to CQC as the incoming provider, the NHS contract needs a novation agreed by the commissioner, and TUPE usually applies to employed staff.

Share purchase

You buy the company that runs the practice. The company stays the same, so CQC says no sale and transfer application is needed, but you must notify CQC of new directors or a new nominated individual and update the statement of purpose. The company keeps its NHS contract, though its change of control terms may need you to tell the commissioner. You also take on the company’s history, good and bad.

1
Regulation

CQC: you need your own registration.

Your registration

  • Your application as the incoming provider, sent at the same time as the seller’s
  • Enhanced DBS check in hand before you apply (under 12 months old)
  • Statement of purpose ready for the practice

CQC sale and transfer guidance

Registered manager

  • A suitable registered manager in place where one is required
  • If the current manager stays: a “continue under a new provider” form
  • If not: the new manager’s application sent with yours

CQC: buy or take over a regulated service

The practice’s record

  • Latest CQC inspection report read on cqc.org.uk
  • Any requirement or warning notices, and what was done
  • The evidence behind the five key questions checked on site

cqc.org.uk · Our CQC inspection checklist

Ask the seller
  • “Can we agree the completion date now, so both CQC applications go in together?”
  • “Is the registered manager staying on?”
  • “Is anything from your last inspection still open?”
Walk away or renegotiate if there are open enforcement actions with no plan, or the seller won’t share the audits and records behind their last inspection.
2
If there is one

The NHS contract: read every year of it.

The contract itself

  • GDS contract or PDS agreement, and who holds it
  • Contracted UDAs (and UOAs for orthodontics) and the contract value
  • How it will move to you: novation, partnership change or share purchase

NHS England Policy book for primary dental services

Delivery history

  • Three years of year-end reconciliation letters
  • Any year under 96% of contracted activity, and money recovered
  • Any breach or remedial notices

Policy book, chapter 8

Your commissioner

  • Your local ICB (the commissioner) contacted early
  • Any conditions they set before they agree a novation
  • The practice is ready for the 2026/27 contract changes, including urgent care

Policy book, chapter 5 · NHS England 2026 reforms guidance

Ask the seller
  • “May I see the last three year-end letters?”
  • “Has any money been clawed back, or is any still owed?”
  • “Who delivers most of the UDAs, and are they staying?”
Walk away or renegotiate if delivery has been under 96% for three years running. NHS England says commissioners should then seek to reduce the contract, and a novation can come with a lower activity level.
3
The team

People: the practice walks out every evening.

Registrations

  • Every dentist, nurse, hygienist and therapist checked on the GDC register
  • Indemnity in place for every registrant
  • Staff files complete (DBS, references, immunity where needed)

GDC register · Regulation 19

Employed staff and TUPE

  • Employee information from the seller at least 4 weeks before the transfer
  • Contracts, pay, holiday and pension terms you’ll take on
  • Any grievances, disciplinaries or claims in the last 2 years

GOV.UK: TUPE

Associates

  • Every associate agreement read: split, notice period, lab bills
  • Who is employed and who is self-employed
  • Restrictions in place if an associate leaves

Your dental solicitor

Ask the seller
  • “Who has told you they might leave?”
  • “How long will you stay on to hand patients over?”
  • “Is anyone on a contract that differs from the rest of the team?”
Walk away or renegotiate if the clinicians who produce most of the income have no agreement in writing, or the seller won’t agree to stay for a handover.
4
Bricks, kit and X-rays

Premises: the surgery you’ll stand in.

The lease

  • Years left, rent and the next rent review
  • Landlord’s consent to assign the lease to you
  • Repair and dilapidations terms, and permitted use

Your dental solicitor and surveyor

Equipment

  • An equipment list: age, ownership, finance still owing
  • Service and validation records for autoclaves and washer-disinfectors
  • Written scheme of examination for the compressor and autoclaves

HTM 01-05 · PSSR 2000

X-rays

  • HSE registration for the X-ray work, and whether you need your own
  • Radiation Protection Adviser, local rules and a radiation file in order
  • Equipment tests and X-ray audits up to date

IRR17 · IR(ME)R 2017

Ask the seller
  • “Has the landlord agreed in principle to the assignment?”
  • “What needs replacing in the next two years?”
  • “Who is your Radiation Protection Adviser?”
Walk away or renegotiate if the lease is short with no right to renew, or key equipment has no service history.
5
Patients and records

Data: the records are the practice.

Patient records

  • Where records are kept: software, paper, X-ray images
  • How they transfer to you on completion, in writing
  • Who keeps older and archived records

UK GDPR · ICO

Your ICO fee

  • Data protection fee paid by the buying legal entity
  • Software and suppliers that process data, with contracts
  • Any data breaches in the last few years

ICO data protection fee

NHS data

  • Data Security and Protection Toolkit status, if there is an NHS contract
  • Patient list size and how many attended in the last 2 years

NHS England

Ask the seller
  • “How many patients came in during the last two years?”
  • “Have you had a data breach or an ICO complaint?”
Walk away or renegotiate if patient numbers can’t be shown from the software, or records aren’t backed up.
6
The numbers

Money: what you’re really paying for.

Fee income

  • Three years of accounts, plus the latest months
  • Fee income split by NHS, private and any membership scheme
  • Income by clinician, so you know who brings it in

Your dental accountant

Costs

  • Associate costs and lab bills
  • Staff costs after TUPE, and any pay rises promised
  • Rent, finance and any one-off costs

Your dental accountant

Goodwill

  • An independent valuation by a specialist dental valuer
  • A restrictive covenant on the seller, agreed in the heads of terms
  • A handover plan so patients stay with the practice

Your dental solicitor and valuer

Ask the seller
  • “How much of the income is your own clinical work?”
  • “What would a new owner need to spend in year one?”
Walk away or renegotiate if the profit only works because the seller does most of the dentistry, and the price doesn’t allow for replacing them.

How is a dental practice valued? In plain words.

Most of what you pay for is goodwill: the patients, the reputation and the income that comes with them. Writing in BDJ In Practice, the British Dental Association’s business journal, specialists describe two common approaches.

  • A multiple of profit. The valuer works out the practice’s sustainable profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation (often called EBITDA), then applies a multiple. The owner’s own dentistry should be costed as if an associate did it, or two practices with the same income can look very different.
  • A percentage of fees. Goodwill is expressed as a share of the practice’s gross fee income, often looked at separately for NHS, private and mixed practices.

Is there a reliable rule of thumb? Not one we’d stake your money on. Published multiples and percentages move with the market and date quickly, and the practices behind an average may look nothing like yours. Get an independent valuation from a specialist dental valuer, and have your accountant test the numbers behind it.

Questions buyers ask us.

What should I check before buying a dental practice?

Before buying a dental practice in England, check six areas with a specialist dental solicitor and accountant. First, regulation: you will need your own CQC registration as the incoming provider, plus a registered manager where one is required, and you should read the practice's latest CQC report. Second, the NHS contract if there is one: how it will move to you, three years of delivery against contracted UDAs, and any money clawed back or breach notices. Third, people: GDC registration for every clinician, TUPE for employed staff, and every associate agreement. Fourth, premises: the lease, the equipment and the X-ray set-up. Fifth, patients and data: how records transfer and your own ICO data protection fee. Sixth, money: fee income, associate and staff costs, and an independent valuation of the goodwill.

Does CQC registration transfer when you buy a dental practice?

No, not when you buy the business itself. If you buy a dental practice's business and assets, you become a new provider and must register with CQC yourself, usually through CQC's sale and transfer process. You and the seller send your applications at the same time or as close together as possible, and the seller must keep providing care until CQC confirms you are registered. CQC keeps the buyer's application open for up to 3 months to allow the sale to complete. You also need a suitable registered manager where one is required: if the current manager stays, they send a form to continue under the new provider. If you buy the shares of a company that is already registered, the registered provider stays the same, so CQC says no sale and transfer application is needed, but you must notify CQC of new directors or a new nominated individual and update the statement of purpose. See our CQC dental inspection checklist.

Does the NHS dental contract transfer to the buyer?

Not automatically. NHS England's Policy book for primary dental services says that replacing one contract holder with another, for example when a practice is sold, is a contract novation: the old contract ends and a new one starts on the same terms. Novation is not a right. It is at the discretion of the commissioner, which is usually the local integrated care board (ICB). Before agreeing, the commissioner can look at past underperformance, money owed back, breach or remedial notices and any open CQC issues, and it can reduce the contracted activity to a realistic level. Partnership changes follow a separate variation process. If you buy the shares of a company that holds the contract, the contract stays with the company, though its change of control terms may require you to tell the commissioner. Speak to your commissioner and a dental solicitor early.

What is the rule of thumb for valuing a dental practice?

There is no reliable rule of thumb for valuing a dental practice. Published multiples and percentages move with the market, date quickly and are averages of practices that may look nothing like the one you are buying. The value depends on sustainable profit, the NHS and private mix, who does the dentistry and whether they are staying, the lease, the condition of the premises and equipment, and the local market. Get an independent valuation from a specialist dental valuer, and have your accountant test the figures behind it before you agree a price.

How are dental practices valued in the UK?

Most of the price of a dental practice is goodwill: the patients, reputation and income that come with the business. In correspondence published in BDJ In Practice, the British Dental Association's business journal, specialists describe two common approaches. One applies a multiple to the practice's sustainable profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, often called EBITDA, where the owner's own clinical work should be costed as if an associate did it. The other expresses goodwill as a percentage of the practice's gross fee income, often looked at separately for NHS, private and mixed practices. Which approach suits depends on the practice, which is why a specialist dental valuer and accountant are needed.

Do staff transfer when you buy a dental practice?

Usually yes, if you buy the business rather than the company. Under TUPE, the Transfer of Undertakings (Protection of Employment) Regulations, employees' jobs usually move to the new employer on the same terms, with their continuous employment kept. GOV.UK says the identity of the employer must change for TUPE to apply to a business transfer, so in a share purchase the staff stay employed by the same company. The seller must give the buyer information about the employees at least 4 weeks before the transfer, including their main terms, and any disciplinaries, grievances or legal claims in the last 2 years. Associates are often self-employed, so read each associate agreement separately with your solicitor.

Can you buy a dental practice if you are not a dentist?

It depends on how the practice is owned. Under section 43 of the Dentists Act 1984, a company that carries on the business of dentistry commits an offence if a majority of its directors are not registered dentists or registered dental care professionals. NHS contracts also have their own eligibility conditions for who can hold them. A buyer who is not a registered healthcare professional also needs a CQC countersigned enhanced DBS check to register with CQC, which CQC says can take up to 60 working days. Take advice from a specialist dental solicitor before you make an offer.

How long does it take to buy a dental practice?

There is no fixed timeline, because it depends on due diligence, finance, the landlord, the NHS commissioner and CQC. A few dates are fixed, though. CQC will not accept a registration application without an enhanced DBS check, rejects any check over 12 months old, and says a CQC countersigned check can take up to 60 working days. CQC keeps a buyer's sale and transfer application open for up to 3 months to allow completion. Under TUPE, the seller must give employee information at least 4 weeks before the transfer. If an NHS contract is involved, the commissioner must agree the change before it can happen. Starting the DBS check, the CQC application and the commissioner conversation early saves the most time.

What is the difference between an asset purchase and a share purchase?

In an asset purchase you buy the business and its assets, such as goodwill, equipment and the lease, from the seller. The provider changes, so you register with CQC as the incoming provider, any NHS contract needs a novation agreed by the commissioner, and TUPE usually applies to employed staff. In a share purchase you buy the company that runs the practice. The company stays the same, so its CQC registration and NHS contract stay with it, though you must notify CQC of changes such as new directors and may need to tell the NHS commissioner under the contract's change of control terms. You also take on the company's history, including any past liabilities, which is why share purchases need especially careful due diligence. Your dental solicitor and accountant will advise which suits you.

Does this checklist apply in Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland?

No. This checklist covers practices in England. Dental practices are regulated by Healthcare Inspectorate Wales in Wales, by Healthcare Improvement Scotland and the NHS boards in Scotland, and by the Regulation and Quality Improvement Authority in Northern Ireland, and each nation runs its own NHS dental arrangements. Employment law such as TUPE, data protection under UK GDPR and the GDC register apply across the UK, but the registration steps and NHS contract rules are different, so take local advice.
